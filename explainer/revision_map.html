Black–Scholes for the Land · revision for AgriAI @ AAAI-27

What the reviewers hit, and what the tests found

The reviewers liked the idea. Every serious critique was about a claim that ran ahead of its evidence. We ran a test for each one, written down before it ran. The core survived. What changes is the fence around each claim, and most fences cost two or three sentences.

Rule for the rewrite

The paper keeps its spine: the idea, the model that works, attribution, and the Greeks. Each fix gets one or two sentences in the main text and points to the appendix for detail. No fix gets to bury the framing.

  • Carbon credits. SNAP's baseline sees only mean annual rain, so weather swings can show up as management gains or losses, and communities gain or lose credit for weather.
  • Index insurance. Payouts follow NDVI against the pixel's own history, so browning from overgrazing can trigger the same payout as browning from drought.
  • The 2023 controversy over the Northern Kenya project, which the paper already cites, questioned both its baseline and how pastoralist communities were treated.

The critiques make this angle stronger. Transparency means saying when the split can be trusted, and the new tests give that answer in numbers (C1): trust it at project scale, and only if management does not itself react to rain. If herders move cattle when rain fails, which is often the sensible thing to do, the model can overstate their effect in that drought by up to about half. A fair system has to say that out loud, and the revised paper will.

One caution on wording. Rain-adjusted baselines (RESTREND) date from 2004, so “not previously possible” would invite a fight. The defensible claim is a split every 16 days at every pixel, with uncertainty and a stated validity range. Annual linear baselines cannot give that.

Proposed wording · not in the paper yet
Approach, end of first paragraph: “The split is also a question of fairness: a baseline blind to rain charges pastoralists for drought and credits them for good rains, rewarding the weather instead of what they did.”
Conclusion: “Accurate attribution decides whether herders are paid for their management or for their weather; stating where it holds is part of making it fair.”

C1

“The residual is not management”

R1, R3 · threatens attribution · R3's whole review
What they said

The residual holds everything the model leaves out. That includes grazing and fire, but also rain-data error, model error, satellite noise and heat. The counterfactual also assumes, without saying so, that the residual would stay the same if the rain changed.

In plain terms: if herders bring cattle in because the rain failed, rain caused that “management” signal. Holding it fixed while swapping in normal rain then gives the wrong answer.

What we tested

Plant a known signal. Treat the fitted model as the true world, so any error comes from the assumption alone. Add a fake management effect we control (−0.01 NDVI per 16 days, on a random half of 2 km blocks) to all 13 held-out seasons. Invert, rerun under normal rain, and compare with the truth.

Corrupt the rain data by 25% and 50% per CHIRPS cell, then check real-data diagnostics: the residual against rain, against pixel quality, and against a second rain product (ERA5).

What we found

  • A surprise. There is an 18% overstatement even when the assumption holds. The residual is stored in units of the model's spread, and the spread grows with rain, so rerunning a drought under normal rain stretches each residual.
  • Scale matters. 25% rain-data error moves the box average by at most 0.005 NDVI but single pixels by 0.03. Attribution is a project-scale product.
  • The headline survives. The 2022 normal-rain counterfactual is −0.099. It becomes −0.096, −0.094 or −0.087 under each correction, so “0.10 below” holds to about 0.01.
What changes

Call it a rain-conditioned residual. Add about 4 lines, “What the counterfactual assumes”: (A1) management does not react to rain; (A2) data errors average out over the area; (A3) the state carries what rain does. Add about 8 lines, “Is the counterfactual identified?”, with the numbers above.

C2

“The baselines are weak”

R2 · threatens “the model works” · half of R2's review
What they said

History alone, a linear model and a hand-made S-curve are easy to beat. Show M2 against strong modern models that also see rain.

What we tested

GBM: gradient-boosted trees given three lags of greenness and six windows of rain. GRU-M2: M2 plus a memory unit, the same model with more capacity, which also tests whether the current-state-only design is enough.

  • Why: memory is not the reason. Given exactly M2's inputs, the trees still win. M2's rollouts drift browner, by 0.01 to 0.02 NDVI over three months. That comes from how M2 is fitted (one step at a time), not from what it sees.

What changes

Put both baselines in the main table. Add about 3 lines saying who wins where and why (drift), and one line naming the fix: fit on multi-step rollouts, as future work.

C3

“I can't find the headline numbers”

R1 · threatens “the model works”
What they said

The abstract claims 23% and 13%. The only table shows one-step skill (0.085), so neither number appears anywhere.

What we found

Both are three-month numbers, and the saved runs reproduce them exactly: 0.226 skill against history, and M2's error 13% below the S-curve's.

What changes

One main-text table with one-step and three-month results for every model, including C2's baselines. The caption gets R1's point: “attribution given the rain that fell, not forecasting.”

C4

“Rain is the only driver”

R2, R1 · touches the model and the idea
What they said

Does the method take other weather, such as heat? Does leaving it out change what the residual means?

What we tested

Refit M2 with ERA5 temperature, evaporative demand and air dryness (vapour-pressure deficit), alone and together with rain.

What we found
  • Rain is the driver. Temperature alone does worse than history. pass
  • Adding heat hurts on later years. 2019–25 ran 0.5–0.6 °C warmer than the training years, and the network extrapolates badly. Inside the training climate the extra drivers help. fail
  • The residual depends on the drivers you include. With heat modelled, the 2022 counterfactual moves from −0.099 to −0.158, because heat leaves the residual. A counterfactual has to say which drivers it sets to normal.
What changes

Two sentences in the main text, with the table in the appendix. They also widen the idea: nothing in the model is specific to rain.

C5

“Satellite gaps are not handled”

R1 · touches “the model works”
What they said

MODIS has gaps, for example 61 days after 1 November 2021, and the paper says “not yet excluded”. Could gaps drive the drought results?

What we found
  • Dropping gap-spanning steps raises every score: three-month skill on Box A goes from 0.226 to 0.269. The 2022 season contains no gap.
  • We also found an unlisted 144-day gap from June 2025. There are 13 held-out seasons, not 14.
What changes

One appendix line, and correct 14 to 13. Keep the lower numbers as the headline, because they are the conservative ones.

C6

“Δ is oversold”

R1 · threatens the Greeks
What they said

The abstract says Δ “can be hedged, allowing efficient pricing”. But your own pre-registered main test of Δ failed on Box A.

What we tested

Re-ran the pre-registered tests after removing the three starting points whose outcome spans the 2021 satellite gap. This is the data fix R1 asked for, decided before the run.

What we found
  • Main test (does Δ predict which windows green more, within each pixel): fail as registered; pass without the gap steps, +.046 [+.014, +.078].
  • Δ sorts patches across the landscape: the top third greens about twice as much as the bottom third. pass
  • Hedging skill (does Δ beat a constant for prediction): fail on both boxes.
What changes

Drop “can be hedged, allowing efficient pricing”. Keep “exposure”: Δ is an exposure map. Report the registered failure first and the gap-free pass as a correction, in one sentence.

The two proposed tests, and the weakness each closes

TLDR. The fence-line test closes the biggest hole left: nothing yet shows that the residual tracks real management on real land. The foundation-model baseline heads off the question every 2026 time-series reviewer asks. Run the fence-line test first.

Test 1 · fence-line and invasion check

Does the residual see management that actually happened?

Shores up
C1 and pillar 3, attribution. The planted-signal test shows the method recovers management if management is there. It cannot show that the real residual contains real management. R1 asked for independent management data and we had none. This test is that data.
Idea
Compare the residual on properties with documented management events against matched neighbouring land. Box B (held out in every year) holds Mpala, Ol Jogi, Loisaba and part of Suyian, all named in the 2017 drought invasions, when roughly 100,000 cattle moved onto private land from January to March. Box A holds Lewa, Borana and Il Ngwesi, and 2022 is a held-out year there.
Pre-registered prediction
On invaded properties the residual falls during the invasion windows, relative to matched land across the boundary, and recovers afterwards. Normal-rain seasons show no gap.
Why AgriAI cares
“Field validation” is in their call. It also makes the fairness argument concrete: the model tells management regimes apart on the ground.
Data
OpenStreetMap polygons exist for every named property (ODbL licence). WDPA holds only forest reserves here. Cross-check the outlines against the Laikipia Wildlife Forum or NRT maps.
Pitfalls
Invasions hit some properties and not others, so code management per property and per year, not by tenure type. Drop pixels within about 500 m of a boundary. Mask cropland at Timau. Elephants browse harder inside conservancies, so wildlife is in the residual too. Box A 2017 is a training year.
Cost
About 2 days. It could fail, and should be reported either way.
Test 2 · zero-shot foundation model

Why not a foundation model?

Shores up
C2 and pillar 2, “the model works”. It answers the reflex question before a reviewer asks it. AgriAI's call names time-series foundation models; organiser Arjunan works on them; and last year's AgriAI ran a zero-shot Sundial forecast of leaf area index (Zhang et al., UConn).
Idea
Chronos-2 (Amazon, Oct 2025, Apache-2.0) takes rain as a built-in covariate, so it can be run twice: history only, a strong M0; and with rain, a strong rain-aware rival. Score both on the same one-step and three-month error metric (CRPS).
Pre-registered prediction
History-only lags M2 at three months, because rain matters. With rain it may come close to M2 or beat it, and it still yields no per-step residual and no structural state to rerun under normal rain.
Risk
It may beat M2 outright, much like GBM on Box B. That is fine if the paper's claim stays “invertible and differentiable”, not “best forecaster”. Sundial needed 512+ steps of history to win in the LAI paper, and a 2019 start gives about 370 here.
Cost
About a day: roughly 4–8 GPU-hours for 25,600 pixels × 150 starting points.
