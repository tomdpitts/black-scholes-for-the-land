\begin{abstract}
Carbon credits and index insurance for grazing rest on counterfactuals, and both need to separate what the weather did to the land from what its managers did. Incumbent baselines either ignore rain, scoring greenness against the pixel's own history, or regress on annual rainfall. We fit a small probabilistic world model of MODIS greenness conditioned on CHIRPS rain and ERA5 evaporative demand over two $45\times45$\,km savanna regions in Laikipia, Kenya, and use it, conceptually, as Black--Scholes is used: inverted on observed greenness it yields an implied residual, i.e. the land's weather-adjusted response; rolled forward under other drivers it can generate counterfactuals; and differentiated with respect to its drivers and state it gives sensitivities, close analogues of the Black--Scholes Greeks. In the 2022 drought, a held-out year, the land ended $0.168$ NDVI below normal. The model attributes $0.105$ to the weather and $0.055$ to the residual, where the z-score insurers use would assign all $0.168$ to the land. Swapping the model's backbone for Chronos-2, a 120M-parameter time-series foundation model, we also independently obtain an attribution of $0.102$. With 5,010 parameters our model matches or beats Chronos-2's forecast skill, and splits the season in $0.07$\,s on a laptop CPU against $11$ minutes on an NVIDIA H100 GPU. Its sensitivities are the clearest gain. On unseen years, the $\Delta$ of our model (i.e. the derivative of greenness with respect to rain) has an error of $22\%$ on average across 2.2\,km blocks, close to the $19\%$ year-to-year noise floor, while Chronos-2's error is $80\%$. Our invertible transition-density world model attributes the weather's share for index insurance and the land's for carbon credits, and gives both an exposure to rain at every pixel.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Various methodologies exist to measure the carbon capture attributable to sustainable grassland management practices, such as rotation of grazing animals, replanting strategies, and backburning. The Northern Kenya Rangelands Carbon Project, the world's largest soil carbon removal project, earns one credit per tonne of CO$_2$ stored in soil above a baseline set by VM0032, a pioneering grazing methodology \citep{vm0032,verra2023}. However, that baseline was contested enough for Verra to suspend issuance for eight months in 2023 \citep{verra2023}. Its baseline model, SNAP \citep{ritchie2014,nkgcp_pd2016}, uses only mean annual rainfall, rather than an input such as CHIRPS 16-day periodic rainfall \citep{chirps} or even seasonal rain records. A farmer who changes nothing can then be penalised for the effects of dry seasons they did not cause, or rewarded for wet seasons. Elsewhere, livestock index insurance in the same counties pays on seasonal NDVI against the insured area's own history \citep{deoto2019}. For maximum efficiency, the insurer ideally would want to quantify forage loss attributed only to factors specifically included in the policy, such as drought, rather than management decisions such as overgrazing. Both categories of payment would benefit from a clean split of the land's response between the climatic conditions and other remaining factors. To the best of our knowledge, no current operational baseline makes that split cleanly. 

In this work we propose a model that makes that split with climate factors as its conditioning input, specifically, CHIRPS rain and ERA5 evaporative demand (ED) \citep{hersbach2020}, two key drivers of the climatic water balance \citep{spei} in these rangelands. Our model is a world model in the sense of the memory model M of \citet{ha2018}: a predictive model of the future that outputs a probability density function. (Since the NDVI state is low-dimensional, no vision component (V) is required to compress raw pixels or similar.)
\begin{alignat*}{4}
\text{\citet{ha2018}, M:}\quad & p_\theta(z_{t+1} &&{}\mid z_t,\; && h_t,\; && \mathrm{act}_t)\\
\text{Ours:}\quad & p_\theta(a_{t+1} &&{}\mid a_t,\; && w_t,\; && u_{t+1})
\end{alignat*}
Our model estimates the specific terrain response from data as a transition density for the greenness anomaly $a$ in place of their learned latent state $z$, given a water-balance memory $w$ of past climatic water balance rather than a recurrent memory $h$, and its current weather value $u$ in place of the agent's action $\mathrm{act}_t$. We also show that adding static attributes \citep{kratzert2019} materially improves the model's skill in forecasts over single-step (16 days) and 3-month timeframes, by adding in topography in the form of Copernicus DEM GLO-30 elevation, slope, and topographic position \citep{copdem}.

The Black--Scholes formula (BSF) \citep{bs} prices an option from observables (the stock price, strike, time to expiry and interest rate) and one parameter that cannot be observed, the volatility. By instead inputting the actual market price, the model can produce that unobservable ``implied'' volatility. We use our model in a similar way. Its observables are the land's current state and the rain and ED that fell, and inverted on the observed greenness, it returns the implied residual, the part of the land's response the weather does not explain.

Our contributions are threefold: (i) the \textbf{implied residual}, i.e. the land's weather-adjusted response. (ii) Rolled forward from the same state under different weather, the model returns the land expected under that \textbf{counterfactual} scenario. This allows us to then split a season into a baseline under normal weather, the effect of the weather that fell, and the residual (Figure~\ref{fig:split}). (iii) Finally, as with the BSF, differentiating the model generates the \textbf{Greeks} of the land's greenness: its sensitivities to rain, evaporative demand and the current state, by automatic differentiation through the rollout.

Separating climate from land use in vegetation records is an old problem. Residual trend analysis (RESTREND) regresses annual or growing-season NDVI on accumulated rainfall and reads trends in the residual as human-induced degradation \citep{evans2004}. Vegetation sensitivity indices add a one-month memory term \citep{seddon2016}. These are linear in their drivers and fitted pixel by pixel at annual or monthly scale. M1 below is a 16-day version, and M2 replaces the regression with a learned, state-dependent transition density shared across pixels. In East Africa, NDVI tracks the rain of the current and two preceding months, with a response that has been found to flatten in wetter areas \citep{davenport1993}. Livestock insurance in Kenya scores seasonal NDVI against the insured area's own history as a z-score \citep{vrieling2014,deoto2019}, which reads the whole anomaly as the land's. Probabilistic deep forecasters and time-series foundation models output predictive densities \citep{chronos2}, and one has forecast leaf area index zero-shot \citep{zhang2025}. To the best of our knowledge, none are built to be inverted on observations or re-run under another driver path.

\section{Method}
\paragraph{Data} The target $a_t$ is greenness, the normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI) from the MODIS satellite product MOD13Q1 \citep{mod13}, at $250$\,m every 16 days (one composite), 2002--2025, as an anomaly against each pixel's 2003--2018 climatology. The z-score, the incumbent industry standard, is this anomaly divided by the pixel's standard deviation for that time of year. (N.B. in this work, we quote the z-score as the anomaly alone, in NDVI, for direct comparison to our model.) The input $r_t$ is rain from CHIRPS \citep{chirps} summed over the same 16-day windows, in units of $100$\,mm; $e_t$ is ED, FAO-56 reference evapotranspiration \citep{allen1998} from ERA5 \citep{hersbach2020}, likewise summed, as an anomaly against 2003--2018. The setting is two $45$\,km savanna regions in Laikipia, Kenya, with seasonal rains typically in March--May and October--December: Box A (development) and Box B (held-out landscape) to its west (Figure~\ref{fig:map}, Appendix). We fit on Box A 2003--2017, early-stop on 2018, and evaluate on Box A 2019--2025. This evaluation period is 13 seasons in total, including a drought in 2021--22 and the subsequent 2023 recovery, and on Box B for the same 2019--2025 timeframe.

\paragraph{Model} Each step sees only the current state, not the series i.e. Markovian. The state is the anomaly $a_t$ and an antecedent index $w_t$ of the climatic water balance, a one-parameter bucket whose memory $\lambda(x)$ depends on the pixel's traits:
\begin{align}
w_{t+1} &= \lambda(x)\,w_t + u_{t+1}, \nonumber\\
a_{t+1} &= a_t + \mu_\theta(a_t, \bar a_t, w_t, u_{t+1}, \phi_{t+1}, x) \nonumber\\
&\quad + \sigma_\theta(\cdot)\,\varepsilon_t,\qquad \varepsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1),
\label{eq:model}
\end{align}
where $t$ indexes 16-day steps, $\bar a_t$ is the $5\times5$ neighbourhood mean of $a_t$, $\phi_{t+1}$ is the seasonal phase (sine and cosine of day of year), $x$ is six static traits of the pixel: mean NDVI, seasonal amplitude, climatological annual rain, and elevation, slope and topographic position from the Copernicus GLO-30 DEM \citep{copdem}, and $u_t=r_t-k(x)\,e_t$ is rain net of ED, the climatic water balance \citep{spei}, with $k$ linear in the traits. $w_t$ is a decaying sum of past $u_t$ that keeps a fraction $\lambda(x)=\mathrm{sigmoid}(c_0+c^\top x)$ each step. $\mu_\theta$ is the expected change in the anomaly and $\sigma_\theta$ its spread, both functions of the same six inputs, so $p_\theta(a_{t+1}\mid\cdot)=\mathcal N(a_t+\mu_\theta,\sigma_\theta^2)$; $\theta$ is one set of weights shared by every pixel. \textbf{M0} uses history alone. \textbf{M1} has $\mu$ and $\log\sigma$ linear in the features. \textbf{M2} adds a 64-unit one-hidden-layer network to $\mu$ and $\log\sigma$. \textbf{M3} adds the topographic traits to M2. \textbf{M4} adds ED ($k=0$ below M4) to M3 and is an equal-weight mixture of five fits. All are fitted by full-batch Adam on the one-step Gaussian log-likelihood of observed transitions.

\paragraph{Residual} Inverting \eqref{eq:model} on an observed transition gives the implied (standardised) residual,
\begin{equation}
\hat\varepsilon_t = \frac{a_{t+1} - a_t - \mu_\theta(a_t,\bar a_t,w_t,u_{t+1},\phi_{t+1},x)}{\sigma_\theta(\cdot)},
\label{eq:implied}
\end{equation}
which in practical terms is simply one number per pixel per 16 days, in units of the model's predicted standard deviation, $\sigma_\theta$. Each step is a one-layer conditional affine flow \citep{papamakarios2021}, so the model is invertible: each observed transition has a unique residual. Here $F_h$ is \eqref{eq:model} rolled forward from the season's starting state with zero residual along the observed rain and ED. Carried autoregressively through the rollout, the implied residuals reproduce the observed path exactly, so their combined effect is the residual term for the season, $a_{t+h}-F_h$, in NDVI rather than units of $\sigma_\theta$. We produce an interval for $F_h$ by re-running the season with residuals resampled from the training seasons (Figure~\ref{fig:attribution}). 


\paragraph{Counterfactual} 

Rolled along any other weather path $(r',e')$, $u'=r'-k(x)\,e'$, the same rollout gives $F_h(r',e')$, so $F_h=F_h(r,e)$. With $(\bar r,0)$, $\bar r$ being the climatological rain, each pixel's 2003--2018 mean for that 16-day step of the year, and $0$ a zero ED anomaly, the season-end anomaly splits exactly into baseline, weather and residual,
\begin{equation}
\medmuskip=1.5mu\thickmuskip=2.5mu
a_{t+h}=\underbrace{F_h(\bar r,0)}_{\text{baseline}}+\underbrace{[F_h(r,e)-F_h(\bar r,0)]}_{\text{weather term}}+\underbrace{[a_{t+h}-F_h(r,e)]}_{\text{residual}}.
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
The weather term is divided between rain and ED by Shapley value over the four combinations of observed and normal rain and ED \citep{shapley1953}. 

\paragraph{Sensitivities}
Following the BSF Greeks, we take Jacobian-vector products through the rollout at normal weather: $\Delta_h=\partial F_h(\bar r,0)/\partial r_{t+1}$, greenness $h$ steps ahead per $100$\,mm of rain now. $\Gamma_h=\partial^2F_h(\bar r,0)/\partial r_{t+1}^2$ is a measure of the saturation effect, or rather, how the sensitivity of $\Delta$ itself changes with rain. $\Theta_h=\partial F_h(\bar r,0)/\partial a_t$ is a measure of how long it takes for anomalies or shocks to subside.

\paragraph{Evaluation} Error is the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) \citep{gneiting2007}, the probabilistic generalisation of absolute error, on observed held-out pixel-steps (one pixel at one 16-day composite); skill is $1-\mathrm{CRPS}/\mathrm{CRPS}_{\mathrm{M0}}$. Each model is scored one step (16 days) ahead, and rolled forward 3 months along the observed weather. As the canonical machine-learning baseline we also fit gradient-boosted trees (GBT) \citep{friedman2001} on recent greenness, rain, ED and the pixel's traits, including topography. We also compare our model to Amazon's Chronos-2 \citep{chronos2}, a time-series foundation model representative of the current state of the art. Chronos-2 is given each pixel's full history from 2002 up to the forecast date and, like our models, the rain and ED that actually fell over the forecast window.

\paragraph{Fit}Adding drivers consistently yields increased skill, at both horizons and on both landscapes (Figure~\ref{fig:skill}, Table~\ref{tab:onestep}). For comparison, we run M2 with and without rain, showing that almost all of its skill is predicated on the rain data. Topography (M3) adds $0.8$--$2.7$ points, and ED (M4) a further $0.8$--$0.9$ on unseen years but $3.5$--$5.0$ on the unseen landscape, the more arid of the two boxes, where we theorise evaporative demand to have a higher influence. Along the weather that fell, M4's 3-month error is $27$ points below history and $12$ below RESTREND on unseen years ($27$ and $10$ on the unseen landscape), and it beats GBT in every column. Against Chronos-2, a 120M-parameter foundation model given the same weather, M4 nearly doubles the 16-day skill and comes within a point at 3 months, with 5,010 parameters.

\paragraph{Residual} 

The residual, the land's weather-adjusted response, is computed by rolling the model forward from a given starting state with the true values for rain and ED. The residual at that particular time is the recorded NDVI minus the model's output. In March--May 2022 the land leaves the range ordinary years' residuals give (shading, Figure~\ref{fig:attribution}) only in the last month of the season, while M0, forecasting from history alone, expects recovery and ends above that range.

\textit{Implied residual surface.} The implied residual surfaces, analogous to implied volatility surfaces, are shown for the z-score, RESTREND, and M4 in Figure~\ref{fig:surface} for the unseen years on Box A. Under Black--Scholes, the implied volatility surface would theoretically be perfectly flat. In reality, surfaces often exhibit skew, as well as anomaly spikes and troughs. Likewise, a residual free of weather should be flat along the water-balance axis, and any slant is weather left in it. Across all 141 held-out start dates and horizons up to 3 months, the z-score's residual tilts noticeably with the water balance, and RESTREND's bends in the wet tail due to the mismatch between linear projection and the saturation effect of rain on growth, diverging in the extremes. Meanwhile, M4's lack of slant illustrates the neutrality of its residual relative to weather. 

\textit{Reliability.} Crucially, the M4 residual does not reward wet seasons or penalise dry ones. Across 13 held-out seasons it moves $+0.004$ NDVI per $100$\,mm of climatic water balance (rain minus ED, with $95\%$ interval $-0.005$ to $+0.022$), against $+0.040$ for a z-score (Table~\ref{tab:bias}). RESTREND also shows no detectable bias ($-0.009$, $95\%$ interval $-0.021$ to $+0.016$), but its linearity causes issues at the extremes. In the wettest season, October--December 2019, it overshoots by predicting $+0.262$ NDVI, exceeding the highest box mean observed in its training data by $48\%$, and leaves a $-0.133$ NDVI residual, against $-0.018$ for M4. In other words, M4 is extracting the weather's nonlinear effect on the land.

\paragraph{Counterfactual}

The counterfactual re-runs the same season from the same starting state under normal weather, and is the mechanism by which we can compute attribution per driver. In March--May 2022 rain was $124$\,mm below normal and ED $48$\,mm above, and the box ended $0.168$ NDVI below normal (Figure~\ref{fig:attribution}, Table~\ref{tab:drought}). A z-score, the basis of current livestock index insurance, attributes all of it to the land. M4 attributes $0.105$ to the weather, $0.064$ to rain and $0.041$ to ED, leaving a $-0.055$ residual. Run through the same split with Chronos-2 as the backbone, the weather share is $0.102$, within the $0.091$--$0.114$ range of M4's five fits.

\paragraph{Sensitivities}

\textit{$\Delta$ and $\Gamma$, rain sensitivity.} On the box mean, an extra $100$\,mm of rain raises greenness by $0.072$ NDVI a month later. Per 2.2\,km block on unseen years, M4's $\Delta$ is off the observed response by $22\%$ on average, against $19\%$ for a perfect model given year-to-year noise, $37\%$ for a flat map at the box mean and $80\%$ for Chronos-2 (Figure~\ref{fig:sens}). Recomputed from the current state at each rainy-season step, $\Delta$ also tracks when a patch responds. Within each pixel, steps in the top $\Delta$ third green $2.8$ times as much per $100$\,mm as those in the bottom third (Table~\ref{tab:greeks}). On the unseen landscape the ranking does not hold, within pixels or across them (Table~\ref{tab:greekstests}).

To first order, the weather term of \eqref{eq:split} can alternatively be expressed as the departures from normal weather, weighted by $\Delta$,
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{F_h(r,e)-F_h(\bar r,0)}_{\text{weather term}}\approx\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_s \Delta^{r}_{h,s}\,\delta r_s}_{\text{rain}}+\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_s \Delta^{e}_{h,s}\,\delta e_s}_{\text{ED}},
\label{eq:taylor}
\end{equation}
where $\delta r_s,\delta e_s$ are the departures of rain and ED from normal in window $s$ and $\Delta^r_{h,s}=\partial F_h/\partial r_s$, $\Delta^e_{h,s}=\partial F_h/\partial e_s$ are taken at normal weather from the pre-season state ($\Delta_h=\Delta^r_{h,t+1}$). In every gap-free held-out drought it is within $8\%$ of the full rollout. In wet seasons greenness saturates, and the second-order terms $\Gamma$ are needed: the rain curvature $\Gamma=\partial^2F_h/\partial r^2<0$ along the season's rain departure, in every held-out season, and with them the approximation is within $4\%$ in every season more than $100$\,mm wetter than normal (Table~\ref{tab:taylor}). Having quantified $\Delta$ per pixel for a given season, a contract can define the weather's share of the season through a simple weighted sum of rain and ED departures, with no model run needed.

\textit{$\Theta$ and $\partial\sigma/\partial r$, decay and spread.} The same rollout quantifies how fast a shock to greenness decays, and how much rain widens the spread of outcomes. We leave both to future work.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Limitations} The evidence comes from two $45$\,km boxes in one region and 13 held-out seasons. The residual separates weather from everything else, not management from everything else: it also holds fire, unmodelled weather and sensor noise, so crediting it to management assumes those average out, and it is not yet validated against recorded management. In this work, we aim to improve land response attribution by factoring out key drivers such as water balance, and leave the exploration of adding further drivers to future work.

\paragraph{World model} How can a model with 5,010 parameters match a 120M-parameter pretrained foundation model on skill yet beat it three-fold on $\Delta$? The difference is a structural one between the two paradigms: world models, and time-series forecasters. Residuals and counterfactuals depend on how the model responds to its drivers. This is not the same as being a good forecaster. Good forecasting along the weather that actually occurred does not necessarily require a correct response to each driver. Chronos-2 matches M4 at 3-month skill, yet its $\Delta$ is $80\%$ off the observed response against M4's $22\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:sens}) and its ranking of patches by sensitivity is backwards ($-0.047$ NDVI per $100$\,mm, most minus least sensitive third, against M4's $+0.061$ on the same pixels).

We cannot inspect Chronos-2's internals, but the data suggests why. Across seasons, rain and ED move almost in lockstep (correlation $-0.98$), so a forecaster can lean on either driver, and Chronos-2 does indeed lean on ED, splitting the 2022 drought mostly to ED (Table~\ref{tab:drought}). At a 16-day step however, the correlation is only $-0.5$, and in a typical fortnight rain differs by about $60$\,mm across the box, while ED varies only by about $3$\,mm. In other words, within a given fortnight, ED acts like a constant while rain varies across pixels. M4 is forced to learn a single transition for all $25\,600$ pixels, conditioned on each pixel's terrain and state, so must be able to explain why pixels a few km apart have different outcomes in the same timeframe. A forecaster reading one pixel's series at a time, even its entire recorded history, never sees those differences. That learned response is what a world model is for: the memory component of \citet{ha2018} must respond correctly to the agent's actions, while ours must respond to the weather, not merely describe what usually happens alongside it. With 5,010 parameters, it is able to produce that response on unseen years close to the year-to-year noise limit. 

\section{Conclusion}
Inverting our learned transition-density world model on observed satellite data produces the implied residual, the land's weather-adjusted response, and with it the weather's share, the two sides that carbon credits and index insurance each need. Its counterfactuals separate that share from the land's starting state and break it down by driver, demonstrated here with rain and ED. By differentiation, the model derives each pixel's sensitivity to rain, which ranks real response on unseen years. While run only as a proof of concept on limited data, with various simplifications, we believe invertible transition-density world models have real practical potential for complex real-world temporal systems and welcome further exploration in this space.
