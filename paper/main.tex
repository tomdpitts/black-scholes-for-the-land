\begin{abstract}
Carbon credits, offsets and index insurance rest on counterfactuals: what the land would have done under the same weather without management, or what a bad season cost. Current baselines fail in two ways: some ignore rain, others condition on rain linearly and annually. But the true response lags the water supply, depends on hidden state such as stored water, and responds differently from pixel to pixel. Focussing on grassland pasture and its climatic water balance as a proof of concept, we fit a temporal world model of MODIS greenness conditioned on CHIRPS rain and ERA5 evaporative demand (ED) over two $45\times45$\,km regions in Laikipia, Kenya: a transition density for greenness given recent and current weather. We use it, conceptually, as Black--Scholes is used. Inverted on observed greenness, the model yields an implied residual, everything the land did that the weather does not explain. Two capabilities drop out of this solution: 1) attribution, what the land did beyond the weather; 2) sensitivities, derivatives of the rollout with respect to rain and state, close analogues of the Greeks. On Laikipia test sites, our learned model recovers three-month greenness with $27\%$ less error than a history-only baseline and $12\%$ less than RESTREND, the standard research method, on unseen years. In the 2022 drought the land ended $0.17$ NDVI below normal. The weather accounts for $0.10$ of that, $0.06$ from failed rain and $0.04$ from excess ED, and the residual for $0.06$. Under normal weather it would have ended $0.01$ below. $\Delta$, the derivative of greenness with respect to rain, is computed at every pixel and averages $0.072$ NDVI a month after an extra $100$\,mm of rain. A $\Delta$ at every pixel is an exposure to rain that can be hedged, allowing efficient pricing of contracts and accurate attribution. The deep learning approach we describe here could furthermore be used with other inputs beyond the weather to derive yet more fine-grained attribution and sensitivities.
\end{abstract}

\section{Approach}
Various methodologies exist to measure the carbon capture attributable to sustainable grassland management practices, such as rotation of grazing animals, replanting strategies, and backburning. The Northern Kenya Rangelands Carbon Project, the world's largest soil carbon removal project, earns one credit per tonne of CO$_2$ stored in soil above a baseline set by VM0032, a pioneering grazing methodology \citep{vm0032,verra2023}. However, that baseline was contested enough for Verra to suspend issuance for eight months in 2023 \citep{verra2023,si2023}. Its baseline model, SNAP \citep{ritchie2014,nkgcp_pd2016}, uses only mean annual rainfall, rather than an input such as CHIRPS 16-day periodic rainfall \citep{nkcp} or even seasonal rain records. Elsewhere, livestock index insurance in the same counties pays on seasonal NDVI against the pixel's own history \citep{ibli}. For maximum efficiency, the insurer ideally would want to quantify forage loss attributed only to factors specifically included in the policy such as drought, rather than management decisions such as overgrazing. Both categories of payment would benefit from a clean split between what the land did because of the climate and what it did because of everything else, and to the best of our knowledge, no current operational baseline makes that split cleanly. The model we propose makes that split with climate factors as its conditioning input. In this work we demonstrate the concept with the two terms of the climatic water balance \citep{spei} in these rangelands, CHIRPS rain and ERA5 evaporative demand (ED) \textbf{add ERA5 citation}, estimating the specific terrain response from data as a transition density $p_\theta(a_{t+1}\mid a_t, w_t, u_{t+1})$ for the greenness anomaly $a$ given a memory $w$ of past climatic water balance and its current value $u$. We also show that adding static attributes (TODO cite Kratzert 2019) materially improves the model's skill in forecasts over single-step (16 days) and 3-month timeframes, by adding in topography in the form of Copernicus DEM GLO-30 elevation, slope, and topographic position \textbf{add citation}.

A Black-Scholes formula (BSF) model \citep{bs} is fed observables such as current stock price, strike price, and used to derive a parameter value -- implied volatility -- which cannot be observed directly but is implied by the market's current pricing of options. In our model the observables are the total state, alongside the rainfall and ED, and the model is inverted on observed greenness. What comes out is the residual i.e. what the land did beyond what the climate drivers by themselves explain. Furthermore, specific values can also be plugged back in to the model. Rolled forward from the same state under other weather, it returns the land expected under that weather. A season then splits into a baseline under normal weather, the effect of the weather that fell, and the residual. Derivatives come by automatic differentiation, as the Greeks come from the BSF.

\paragraph{Pixel characteristics}

\paragraph{Residual} We use the term `residual' to encompass any deviation between the climate-adjusted modelled expectation and the observed ground truth. For clarity, this includes the impacts of all other factors such as grazing, clearing, and fire, the management and disturbance signal itself, but also any weather other than rain and ED, which this demonstration does not condition on, as well as noise in data sources. 


\section{Related Work}
Separating climate from land use in vegetation records is an old problem. Residual trend analysis (RESTREND) regresses annual or growing-season NDVI on accumulated rainfall and reads trends in the residual as human-induced degradation \citep{evans2004,wessels2007}; later variants find breakpoints in the residual trend \citep{burrell2017}, and vegetation sensitivity indices add a one-month memory term \citep{seddon2016}. These are linear in their drivers and fitted pixel by pixel at annual or monthly scale. M1 below is a 16-day version; M2 replaces the regression with a learned, state-dependent transition density shared across pixels. In East Africa, NDVI tracks the rain of the current and two preceding months, with a response that has been found to flatten in wetter areas \citep{nicholson1990,davenport1993}. Operational drought monitoring and livestock insurance in Kenya score NDVI against the pixel's own history \citep{kogan1990,klisch2016,vrieling2014,ibli} i.e. the history-only baseline M0. Deep temporal models conditioned on weather already predict crop yield \citep{shook2021}; probabilistic deep forecasters and time-series foundation models output predictive densities \citep{salinas2020,rasul2023,ansari2024}, and one has forecast leaf area index zero-shot \citep{zhang2025}. To the best of our knowledge, none are built to be inverted on observations or re-run under another driver path.

\section{Setup}
\paragraph{Data} The target $a_t$ is greenness, the normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI) from the MODIS satellite product MOD13Q1 \citep{mod13}, at $250$\,m every 16 days (one composite), 2002--2025, as an anomaly against each pixel's 2003--2018 climatology. The z-score used throughout is this anomaly divided by the pixel's standard deviation for that time of year. The input $r_t$ is rain from CHIRPS \citep{chirps} summed over the same 16-day windows, in units of $100$\,mm; $e_t$ is ED, FAO-56 reference evapotranspiration from ERA5 \textbf{add citation}, likewise summed, as an anomaly against 2003--2018. The setting is two $45$\,km regions in Laikipia, Kenya, with rains in March--May and October--December: Box A (development) and Box B (held-out landscape) to its west (Figure~\ref{fig:map}, Appendix). We fit on Box A 2003--2017, early-stop on 2018, and evaluate on Box A 2019--2025, 14 seasons including the 2021--22 drought and 2023 recovery, and on Box B for the same 2019--2025 timeframe.

\paragraph{Model} Each step sees only the current state, not the series i.e. Markovian. The state is the anomaly $a_t$ and an antecedent index $w_t$ of the climatic water balance, a one-parameter bucket whose memory $\lambda(x)$ depends on the pixel's traits:
\begin{align}
w_{t+1} &= \lambda(x)\,w_t + u_{t+1}, \nonumber\\
a_{t+1} &= a_t + \mu_\theta(a_t, \bar a_t, w_t, u_{t+1}, \phi_{t+1}, x) \nonumber\\
&\quad + \sigma_\theta(\cdot)\,\varepsilon_t,\qquad \varepsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1),
\label{eq:model}
\end{align}
where $t$ indexes 16-day steps, $\bar a_t$ is the $5\times5$ neighbourhood mean of $a_t$, $\phi_{t+1}$ is the seasonal phase (sine and cosine of day of year), $x$ is six static traits of the pixel: mean NDVI, seasonal amplitude, climatological annual rain, and elevation, slope and topographic position from the Copernicus GLO-30 DEM \textbf{add citation}, and $u_t=r_t-k(x)\,e_t$ is rain net of ED, the climatic water balance \citep{spei}, with $k$ linear in the traits. $w_t$ is a decaying sum of past $u_t$ that keeps a fraction $\lambda(x)=\mathrm{sigmoid}(c_0+c^\top x)$ each step. $\mu_\theta$ is the expected change in the anomaly and $\sigma_\theta$ its spread, both functions of the same six inputs, so $p_\theta(a_{t+1}\mid\cdot)=\mathcal N(a_t+\mu_\theta,\sigma_\theta^2)$; $\theta$ is one set of weights shared by every pixel. \textbf{M0} uses history alone. \textbf{M1} has $\mu$ and $\log\sigma$ linear in the features. \textbf{M1.5} adds a hand-written S-curve in $w_t+r_{t+1}$, six parameters. \textbf{M2} adds a 64-unit one-hidden-layer network to $\mu$ and $\log\sigma$. \textbf{M3} adds the topographic traits. \textbf{M4} adds ED ($k=0$ below M4) and is an equal-weight mixture of five fits. All are fitted by full-batch Adam \citep{kingma2015} on the one-step Gaussian log-likelihood of observed transitions.

\paragraph{Residual} Inverting \eqref{eq:model} on an observed transition gives the implied (standardised) residual,
\begin{equation}
\hat\varepsilon_t = \frac{a_{t+1} - a_t - \mu_\theta(a_t,\bar a_t,w_t,u_{t+1},\phi_{t+1},x)}{\sigma_\theta(\cdot)},
\label{eq:implied}
\end{equation}
one number per pixel per 16 days, in units of the model's own spread. Accumulated over a season it is the attribution product: $a_{t+h}-F_h$, where $F_h=\mathbb E[a_{t+h}]$ is the mean rollout along the observed rain and ED, with the model's spread as its interval. Rolling \eqref{eq:model} forward from the same state with zero residual along a weather path $(r',e')$, $u'=r'-k(x)\,e'$, gives $F_h(r',e')$. With $(\bar r,0)$ the climatological rain and zero ED anomaly, the season-end anomaly splits exactly into baseline, weather and residual,
\begin{equation}
a_{t+h}=F_h(\bar r,0)+\big[F_h(r,e)-F_h(\bar r,0)\big]+\big[a_{t+h}-F_h(r,e)\big].
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
The weather term is divided between rain and ED by Shapley value over the four combinations of observed and normal rain and ED \textbf{add citation}. Sensitivities (following BSF Greeks) are Jacobian-vector products through the rollout: $\Delta_h=\partial F_h/\partial r_{t+1}$, greenness $h$ steps ahead per $100$\,mm of rain now; $\Theta_h=\partial F_h/\partial a_t$, how much of today's anomaly survives. The anomaly score is $-\log p_\theta(a_{t+1}\mid a_t,w_t,u_{t+1})$ i.e. the surprise of the weather-conditioned model at what the satellite saw. N.B. the residual and the score are (approximately) two readings of the same quantity.

\paragraph{Evaluation} Error is the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) \citep{gneiting2007}, the probabilistic generalisation of absolute error, on observed held-out pixel-steps (one pixel at one 16-day composite); skill is $1-\mathrm{CRPS}/\mathrm{CRPS}_{\mathrm{M0}}$. Each model is scored one step (16 days) ahead, and rolled forward three months along the observed weather.

\paragraph{Fit} M4's one-step skill exceeds M1's by $0.10$ on unseen years and $0.09$ on the unseen landscape. Along the weather that fell, three-month error is $27\%$ below history and $12\%$ below RESTREND on unseen years, $27\%$ and $10\%$ on the unseen landscape (Figure~\ref{fig:response}, Table~\ref{tab:onestep}). Fed climatological rain the same network falls behind history within two months; fitted without rain its skill is $0.016$. The S-curve's amplitude fitted near zero: the nonlinearity is in how rain interacts with pixel, state and season, not in rain alone. \textbf{TODO M2 numbers in the last two sentences, keep as M2 evidence or cut}

\paragraph{Residual and counterfactual} In March--May 2021 rain was $9$\,mm above normal and evaporative demand $13$\,mm above. The weather term is $+0.01$ NDVI and the residual $-0.03$. In March--May 2022 rain was $124$\,mm below normal and evaporative demand $48$\,mm above, and the box ended $0.17$ NDVI below normal (Figure~\ref{fig:attribution}). History alone predicts recovery, so a z-score assigns the whole $0.17$ to the land. From the late-February state the model expects $-0.01$ under normal weather. The rain that failed accounts for $0.06$, excess evaporative demand $0.04$ and the residual $0.06$. The run under the weather that fell is the reference for a credit, and the residual is what management is credited or debited for. The weather term is what an index insurer pays on. The land leaves the range of ordinary non-weather variation only in the last month of the season, and history's recovery ends above that range. What the model leaves unexplained barely leans with the weather. Across 13 held-out seasons it moves $+0.004$ NDVI per $100$\,mm of rain, against $+0.043$ for a z-score (Appendix).

\textbf{$\Delta$ -- } On the grid mean an extra $100$\,mm of rain raises greenness by $0.072$ NDVI a month later. Recomputed from the current state at each rainy-season step, $\Delta$ sorts exposure well on unseen years. Sorted by $\Delta$, the top third of patches green about three times as much per $100$\,mm as the least (Table~\ref{tab:greeks}). On the unseen landscape the ranking does not hold, within pixels or across them (Table~\ref{tab:greekstests}).

\textbf{$\Theta$ and $\partial\sigma/\partial r$ -- } The same rollout gives how fast a shock to greenness decays and how much rain widens the spread of outcomes. We leave both to future work.

\section{Conclusion}
Inverting our learned transition density world model on observed satellite data produces the residual, what the land did beyond the weather's effect. By differentiation, it returns each pixel's sensitivity to rain, which ranks real response on unseen years. In March--May 2022, a held-out year, Box A ended $0.17$ NDVI below normal. The model attributes $0.06$ to the rain that failed, $0.04$ to excess evaporative demand and $0.06$ to the residual. Under normal weather the land would have ended $0.01$ below (Figure~\ref{fig:attribution}). A credit should be measured on the residual and a payout on the weather term. A z-score would predict recovery that season and attribute the whole $0.17$ to management. While only a proof of concept on limited data and with various simplifications, we believe this invertible transition density approach has real potential to practical world modelling of complex real-world temporal systems that has genuine utility.
