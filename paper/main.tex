\begin{abstract}
Carbon credits and index insurance for grazing rest on counterfactuals, and both need to separate what the weather did to the land from what its managers did. Incumbent baselines either ignore rain, scoring greenness against the pixel's own history, or regress on annual rainfall. We fit a small probabilistic world model of MODIS greenness conditioned on CHIRPS rain and ERA5 evaporative demand over two $45\times45$\,km savanna regions in Laikipia, Kenya, and use it, conceptually, as Black--Scholes is used: inverted on observed greenness it yields an implied residual, i.e. what the land did beyond the weather; rolled forward under other drivers it can generate counterfactuals; and differentiated with respect to its drivers and state it gives sensitivities, close analogues of the Black--Scholes Greeks. In the 2022 drought, a held-out year, the land ended $0.17$ NDVI below normal. The model attributes $0.10$ to the weather and $0.06$ to the residual, where the z-score insurers use would assign all $0.17$ to the land. Swapping the model's backbone for Chronos-2, a 120M-parameter time-series foundation model, we also independently obtain an attribution of $0.10$. With 5,010 parameters it matches or beats Chronos-2's forecast skill, and splits the season in $0.07$\,s on a laptop CPU against $11$ minutes on an NVIDIA H100 GPU. Its sensitivities are the clearest gain. On unseen years, the $\Delta$ of our model (i.e. the derivative of greenness with respect to rain) has an error of $22\%$ on average across 2\,km blocks, close to the $19\%$ year-to-year noise floor, while Chronos-2's error is $80\%$. Our invertible world model attributes the weather's share for index insurance and the land's for carbon credits, and gives both an exposure to rain at every pixel.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Various methodologies exist to measure the carbon capture attributable to sustainable grassland management practices, such as rotation of grazing animals, replanting strategies, and backburning. The Northern Kenya Rangelands Carbon Project, the world's largest soil carbon removal project, earns one credit per tonne of CO$_2$ stored in soil above a baseline set by VM0032, a pioneering grazing methodology \citep{vm0032,verra2023}. However, that baseline was contested enough for Verra to suspend issuance for eight months in 2023 \citep{verra2023,si2023}. Its baseline model, SNAP \citep{ritchie2014,nkgcp_pd2016}, uses only mean annual rainfall, rather than an input such as CHIRPS 16-day periodic rainfall \citep{nkcp} or even seasonal rain records. A farmer who changes nothing can then be penalised for the effects of dry seasons they did not cause, or rewarded for wet seasons. Elsewhere, livestock index insurance in the same counties pays on seasonal NDVI against the insured area's own history \citep{ibli,deoto2019}. For maximum efficiency, the insurer ideally would want to quantify forage loss attributed only to factors specifically included in the policy such as drought, rather than management decisions such as overgrazing. Both categories of payment would benefit from a clean split between what the land did because of the climate and what it did because of everything else, and to the best of our knowledge, no current operational baseline makes that split cleanly. 

In this work we propose a model that makes that split with climate factors as its conditioning input, specifically, CHIRPS rain and ERA5 evaporative demand (ED) \citep{hersbach2020}, two key drivers of the climatic water balance \citep{spei} in these rangelands. Our model is a world model in the sense of \citet{ha2018} memory model M: a predictive model of the future that outputs a probability density function. (Since the NDVI state is low-dimensional, no vision component (V) is required to compress raw pixels or similar. )

\begin{alignat*}{4}
\text{\citet{ha2018}, M:}&\quad p_\theta(z_{t+1}&&{}\mid z_t,\;&&h_t,\;&&\mathrm{act}_t)
\end{alignat*}

Our model estimates the specific terrain response from data as a transition density for the greenness anomaly $a$ in place of their learned latent state $z$, given a water-balance memory $w$ of past climatic water balance rather than a recurrent memory $h$, and its current weather value $u$ in place of the agent's action $\mathrm{act}_t$:
\begin{alignat*}{4}
\quad p_\theta(a_{t+1}&&{}\mid a_t,\;&&w_t,\;&&u_{t+1})
\end{alignat*}

We also show that adding static attributes \citep{kratzert2019} materially improves the model's skill in forecasts over single-step (16 days) and 3-month timeframes, by adding in topography in the form of Copernicus DEM GLO-30 elevation, slope, and topographic position \citep{copdem}.

A \citep{bs} model (BSF) is fed observables such as current stock price, strike price, and used to derive a parameter value -- implied volatility -- which cannot be observed directly but is implied by the market's current pricing of options. In our model the observables are the total state, alongside the rainfall and ED, and the model is inverted on observed greenness. 

In this work we present the insights this novel modelling concept yields: (i) the \textbf{implied residual}, i.e. what the land did beyond what the climate drivers by themselves explain. (ii) Plugged back in and rolled forward from the same state under different weather, the model returns the land expected under that \textbf{counterfactual} scenario. This allows us to then split a season into a baseline under normal weather, the effect of the weather that fell, and the residual (Figure~\ref{fig:split}). (iii) Finally, as with the BSF, differentiating the model generates the \textbf{Greeks} of the land's greenness: its sensitivities to rain, evaporative demand and the current state, by automatic differentiation through the rollout.

\section{Related Work}
Separating climate from land use in vegetation records is an old problem. Residual trend analysis (RESTREND) regresses annual or growing-season NDVI on accumulated rainfall and reads trends in the residual as human-induced degradation \citep{evans2004,wessels2007}; later variants find breakpoints in the residual trend \citep{burrell2017}, and vegetation sensitivity indices add a one-month memory term \citep{seddon2016}. These are linear in their drivers and fitted pixel by pixel at annual or monthly scale. M1 below is a 16-day version; M2 replaces the regression with a learned, state-dependent transition density shared across pixels. In East Africa, NDVI tracks the rain of the current and two preceding months, with a response that has been found to flatten in wetter areas \citep{nicholson1990,davenport1993}. Livestock insurance in Kenya scores seasonal NDVI against the insured area's own history as a z-score \citep{vrieling2014,deoto2019}, which reads the whole anomaly as the land's. Deep temporal models conditioned on weather already predict crop yield \citep{shook2021}; probabilistic deep forecasters and time-series foundation models output predictive densities \citep{salinas2020,rasul2023,ansari2024}, and one has forecast leaf area index zero-shot \citep{zhang2025}. To the best of our knowledge, none are built to be inverted on observations or re-run under another driver path.

\section{Method}
\paragraph{Data} The target $a_t$ is greenness, the normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI) from the MODIS satellite product MOD13Q1 \citep{mod13}, at $250$\,m every 16 days (one composite), 2002--2025, as an anomaly against each pixel's 2003--2018 climatology. The z-score, the incumbent industry standard, is this anomaly divided by the pixel's standard deviation for that time of year. (N.B. in this work, we quote the z-score as the anomaly alone, in NDVI, for direct comparison to our model.) The input $r_t$ is rain from CHIRPS \citep{chirps} summed over the same 16-day windows, in units of $100$\,mm; $e_t$ is ED, FAO-56 reference evapotranspiration \citep{allen1998} from ERA5 \citep{hersbach2020}, likewise summed, as an anomaly against 2003--2018. The setting is two $45$\,km savanna regions in Laikipia, Kenya, with rains in March--May and October--December: Box A (development) and Box B (held-out landscape) to its west (Figure~\ref{fig:map}, Appendix). We fit on Box A 2003--2017, early-stop on 2018, and evaluate on Box A 2019--2025, 13 seasons including the 2021--22 drought and 2023 recovery, and on Box B for the same 2019--2025 timeframe.

\paragraph{Model} Each step sees only the current state, not the series i.e. Markovian. The state is the anomaly $a_t$ and an antecedent index $w_t$ of the climatic water balance, a one-parameter bucket whose memory $\lambda(x)$ depends on the pixel's traits:
\begin{align}
w_{t+1} &= \lambda(x)\,w_t + u_{t+1}, \nonumber\\
a_{t+1} &= a_t + \mu_\theta(a_t, \bar a_t, w_t, u_{t+1}, \phi_{t+1}, x) \nonumber\\
&\quad + \sigma_\theta(\cdot)\,\varepsilon_t,\qquad \varepsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1),
\label{eq:model}
\end{align}
where $t$ indexes 16-day steps, $\bar a_t$ is the $5\times5$ neighbourhood mean of $a_t$, $\phi_{t+1}$ is the seasonal phase (sine and cosine of day of year), $x$ is six static traits of the pixel: mean NDVI, seasonal amplitude, climatological annual rain, and elevation, slope and topographic position from the Copernicus GLO-30 DEM \citep{copdem}, and $u_t=r_t-k(x)\,e_t$ is rain net of ED, the climatic water balance \citep{spei}, with $k$ linear in the traits. $w_t$ is a decaying sum of past $u_t$ that keeps a fraction $\lambda(x)=\mathrm{sigmoid}(c_0+c^\top x)$ each step. $\mu_\theta$ is the expected change in the anomaly and $\sigma_\theta$ its spread, both functions of the same six inputs, so $p_\theta(a_{t+1}\mid\cdot)=\mathcal N(a_t+\mu_\theta,\sigma_\theta^2)$; $\theta$ is one set of weights shared by every pixel. \textbf{M0} uses history alone. \textbf{M1} has $\mu$ and $\log\sigma$ linear in the features. \textbf{M1.5} adds a hand-written S-curve in $w_t+r_{t+1}$, six parameters. \textbf{M2} adds a 64-unit one-hidden-layer network to $\mu$ and $\log\sigma$. \textbf{M3} adds the topographic traits to an \textbf{M2} model. \textbf{M4} adds ED ($k=0$ below M4) to an \textbf{M3} and is an equal-weight mixture of five fits. All are fitted by full-batch Adam \citep{kingma2015} on the one-step Gaussian log-likelihood of observed transitions.

\paragraph{Residual} Inverting \eqref{eq:model} on an observed transition gives the implied (standardised) residual,
\begin{equation}
\hat\varepsilon_t = \frac{a_{t+1} - a_t - \mu_\theta(a_t,\bar a_t,w_t,u_{t+1},\phi_{t+1},x)}{\sigma_\theta(\cdot)},
\label{eq:implied}
\end{equation}
which in practical terms is simply one number per pixel per 16 days, in units of the model's predicted standard deviation, $\sigma_\theta$. Carried through the rollout, these residuals aggregate to make up the residual term for the season, $a_{t+h}-F_h$. Here $F_h(r',e')$ is \eqref{eq:model} rolled forward from the season's starting state with zero residual along a weather path $(r',e')$, $u'=r'-k(x)\,e'$, and $F_h=F_h(r,e)$ follows the observed rain and ED. We produce its interval by re-running the season with residuals resampled from the training seasons (Figure~\ref{fig:attribution}). With $(\bar r,0)$ the climatological rain and zero ED anomaly, the season-end anomaly splits exactly into baseline, weather and residual, 

\begin{equation}
a_{t+h}=F_h(\bar r,0)+\big[F_h(r,e)-F_h(\bar r,0)\big]+\big[a_{t+h}-F_h(r,e)\big].
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
The weather term is divided between rain and ED by Shapley value over the four combinations of observed and normal rain and ED \citep{shapley1953}. Sensitivities (following BSF Greeks) are Jacobian-vector products through the rollout: $\Delta_h=\partial F_h/\partial r_{t+1}$, greenness $h$ steps ahead per $100$\,mm of rain now; $\Theta_h=\partial F_h/\partial a_t$, how much of today's anomaly survives.


\paragraph{Evaluation} Error is the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) \citep{gneiting2007}, the probabilistic generalisation of absolute error, on observed held-out pixel-steps (one pixel at one 16-day composite); skill is $1-\mathrm{CRPS}/\mathrm{CRPS}_{\mathrm{M0}}$. Each model is scored one step (16 days) ahead, and rolled forward three months along the observed weather. As the canonical machine-learning baseline we also fit gradient-boosted trees \citep{friedman2001} on recent greenness, rain, ED and the pixel's traits, including topography. We also compare our model to Amazon's Chronos-2 \citep{chronos2}, given each pixel's full history from 2002 up to the forecast date with rain and ED as covariates, known over the forecast as for our models, a time-series foundation model representative of the current state of the art. 

\section{Results}
\paragraph{Fit} Beginning with M1, we seed a small bump in 16 day skill and a larger increase on 3 months relative to a 'History Alone' M0 model by fitting a linear regression on the recorded rainfall against NDVI. Adding a neural network (M2) significantly bolsters performance, although we observe the gain is heavily contingent on the rain input itself. M2 without the rain input drops its skill from $8.5\%$ to $1.6\%$ at 16 days and from $22.6\%$ to $-0.5\%$ at three months, and on the unseen landscape from $8.4\%$ to $1.2\%$ and from $18.8\%$ to $-4.8\%$. Adding the three topographic static attributes in M3 adds $0.9--1.5$ points and $0.8--2.7$ points over M2 on unseen years and landscape respectively. A further $0.8--0.9$ points on unseen years are gained when a second climate driver, ED, is added in M4, while a significantly larger jump of $3.5--5.0$ points is observed on the unseen landscape of Box B. While Box A and Box B are neighbouring, Box B is the more arid of the two, and we theorise that ED is a more significant driver in this region, contributing to the larger gain in skill. All in all, M4's one-step skill exceeds M1's by $9.8$ points on unseen years and $9.1$ on the unseen landscape. Along the weather that fell, three-month error is $27$ points below history and $12$ points below RESTREND on unseen years, $27$ and $10$ points on the unseen landscape (Figure~\ref{fig:skill}, Table~\ref{tab:onestep}).

\paragraph{Residual and counterfactual} March--May 2021 was close to normal weather (rain $9$\,mm above normal, ED $13$\,mm above), so it acts as a pseudo-control: the weather term is only $+0.01$ NDVI, and the $-0.03$ residual is change the weather does not explain. In March--May 2022 rain was $124$\,mm below normal and ED $48$\,mm above, and the box ended $0.17$ NDVI below normal (Figure~\ref{fig:attribution}). A z-score, the basis of current livestock index insurance, assigns the whole $0.17$ to the land. From the late-February state the model expects $-0.01$ under normal weather. The rain that failed accounts for $0.06$, excess ED $0.04$ and the residual $0.06$. The land leaves the range ordinary years' residuals give (shading, Figure~\ref{fig:attribution}) only in the last month of the season, while M0, forecasting from history alone, expects recovery and ends above that range. 

Crucially, the residual does not reward wet seasons or penalise dry ones. Across 13 held-out seasons it moves $+0.004$ NDVI per $100$\,mm of climatic water balance (rain minus ED, with $95\%$ interval $-0.005$ to $+0.022$), against $+0.040$ for a z-score. RESTREND also shows no detectable bias ($-0.009$, $95\%$ interval $-0.021$ to $+0.016$) but its linearity causes issues at the extremes. In the wettest season, October--December 2019, it overshoots by predicting $+0.26$ NDVI, exceeding the highest box mean observed in its training data by $48\%$, and leaves a $-0.13$ NDVI residual, against $-0.02$ for M4. In other words, M4 is extracting the weather's nonlinear effect on the land.

\textbf{$\Delta$ -- } On the box mean an extra $100$\,mm of rain raises greenness by $0.072$ NDVI a month later. Per 2\,km block on unseen years, M4's $\Delta$ is off the observed response by $22\%$ on average, against $19\%$ for a perfect model given year-to-year noise, $37\%$ for a flat map at the box mean and $80\%$ for Chronos-2 (Figure~\ref{fig:sens}). Recomputed from the current state at each rainy-season step, $\Delta$ also tracks when a patch responds: within each pixel, steps in the top $\Delta$ third green $2.8$ times as much per $100$\,mm as those in the bottom third (Table~\ref{tab:greeks}). On the unseen landscape the ranking does not hold, within pixels or across them (Table~\ref{tab:greekstests}).

To first order the weather term of \eqref{eq:split} is the departures from normal weather, weighted by $\Delta$,
\begin{equation}
F_h(r,e)-F_h(\bar r,0)\approx\textstyle\sum_s \Delta^{r}_{h,s}\,\delta r_s+\sum_s \Delta^{e}_{h,s}\,\delta e_s,
\label{eq:explain}
\end{equation}
where $\delta r_s,\delta e_s$ are the departures of rain and ED from normal in window $s$ and $\Delta^r_{h,s}=\partial F_h/\partial r_s$, $\Delta^e_{h,s}=\partial F_h/\partial e_s$ are taken at normal weather from the pre-season state ($\Delta_h=\Delta^r_{h,t+1}$). In every gap-free held-out drought it is within $8\%$ of the full rollout (Table~\ref{tab:explain}). In wet seasons greenness saturates, and the second-order terms $\Gamma$ are needed: the rain curvature $\Gamma_{rr}<0$, i.e. $\partial^2F_h/\partial r^2<0$ along the season's rain departure, in every held-out season, and with them the approximation is within $4\%$ in every season more than $100$\,mm wetter than normal (Table~\ref{tab:explain}).

\textbf{$\Theta$ and $\partial\sigma/\partial r$ -- } The same rollout gives how fast a shock to greenness decays and how much rain widens the spread of outcomes. We leave both to future work.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Comments}

There are some striking parallels between financial markets and Earth Observation. Firstly, in both domains there is an asymmetric and bilateral interaction between individuals and the world (or one might say 'universe'). We are swept along with its turbulent eddys, and in return we change its course to an imperceptible degree. In both domains, the primary interest is the trajectory that did (or will) actually occur - accurate modelling of alternative scenarios is required for risk management.

\paragraph{World model} Residuals, counterfactuals and payouts all depend on how the model responds to its drivers, not on how well it extends the series. In other words, forecast skill is necessary but not sufficient. Chronos-2 matches M4 at three months, yet its $\Delta$ is $80\%$ off the observed response against M4's $22\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:sens}) and its per patch categorisation is backwards, demonstrating . That response is what a world model is for: the memory component of \citet{ha2018} must respond correctly to the agent's actions, while ours responds dynamically to the weather. With 5,010 parameters, it produces that response on unseen years close to the limit set by year-to-year noise.

\paragraph{Limitations} The evidence comes from two $45$\,km boxes in one region and 13 held-out seasons. The residual separates weather from everything else, not management from everything else: it also holds fire, unmodelled weather and sensor noise, so crediting it to management assumes those average out, and it is not yet validated against recorded management. In this work, we aim to improve land response attribution by factoring out water balance drivers, and leave the exploration of adding further drivers to future work.

\section{Conclusion}
Inverting our learned transition density world model on observed satellite data produces the implied residual, what the land did beyond the weather's effect. By differentiation, it returns each pixel's sensitivity to rain, which ranks real response on unseen years. In March--May 2022, a held-out year, Box A ended $0.17$ NDVI below normal. The model attributes $0.06$ to the rain that failed, $0.04$ to excess ED and $0.06$ to the residual. Under normal weather the land would have ended $0.01$ below (Figure~\ref{fig:attribution}). While only a proof of concept on limited data and with various simplifications, we believe this invertible transition density approach has real potential to practical world modelling of complex real-world temporal systems that has genuine utility.
